Flagship case · Onboarding and knowledge transfer · Adult learning

Handing over a system to someone who didn't build it

My role designed the handover: walkthrough, written guide and an ask-anything assistantLearner a new colleague, new to instructional design and to the organisationTools a Markdown guide, an AI assistant built on itTimeline January to September 2026

The situation

I had built the whole system we use to design our Social Science workbook: the written standard, the rules, the checker and the review rounds. Then a new colleague joined, and his job was to build the Maths books the same way.

I walked him through it. It wasn't enough, and that wasn't his fault. He was new to instructional design and new to us. Things that were obvious to me after months of building were invisible to him, and there was simply too much to take in at once.

Too much at once, and the obvious parts were only obvious to me.

What I noticed

Some questions weren't getting asked. When you're new, you don't want to ask the same thing twice, or ask something that sounds basic to the person who built it. Those unasked questions were exactly where he would get stuck later.

The questions he didn't ask were the ones that mattered.

What I designed

I took the system apart, one piece at a time. For each piece I wrote down three things: what it does, why the organisation needs it, and how I think about it when I use it. Not just the steps but the reasoning behind them, so he could apply it to a different subject instead of copying Social Science into Maths.

That became one written guide. Then I turned the guide into a chatbot he could ask anything, at any hour, including the questions he felt shy asking me. Its answers came from the guide, so they followed the same reasoning I had written down.

The design follows what we know about adults learning a complex job: break it into parts so the load is manageable, explain the why so the learning transfers, give support at the moment it's needed, and make it safe to ask.

Steps get copied. Reasons get adapted.

## 4. The independent checker

What it does: reviews each chapter against the written standard before a person does.

Why we need it: the writer is the worst judge of their own chapter. Reviewers' time should go on real problems.

How I think about it: if the checker and I disagree, the standard is probably unclear. Fix the wording, not just the chapter.

Common mistake: treating a pass as "done". It means "ready for a person".

How the handover ran

  1. A live walkthrough of the whole system, for the big picture.
  2. The system broken into parts, each written up with what it does, why it exists and how I think about it.
  3. An ask-anything assistant built on that guide, there whenever he got stuck.
  4. He builds the Maths books with the system.

Looking back

What worked

Writing down the reasoning, not just the steps, and giving him somewhere to ask the shy questions. I would never have heard those questions otherwise.

What I'd change

I'd keep a log of what he asked the assistant. Those questions show exactly where my guide was unclear, and I'd improve the guide from them.

Case 2 · Assessment design · Grade 7 Social Science workbook

The workbook nobody could finish

My role sole designer, from analysis to rulesAudience Grade 7 students and their teachersTimeline October 2026 onwardTools written rules file, Claude Code for answer keys

The problem

Schools were using our 12-chapter workbook alongside the national textbook, and they kept telling us the same thing: there isn't time to do both. The brief I got was to cut it.

Cut what, and by what measure?

What I asked first

Cut what, and measured how? Question count was the obvious target, but a page of one-word blanks and a page with one long map question are not the same load. So I set the target as printed space, and the rule as one to two questions per concept rather than every line of the textbook.

I also ruled two things out. Cutting whole chapters would have left gaps in the syllabus. Rewording questions to make them shorter would have changed what they test. Questions I removed went to a supplementary book instead of disappearing, so teachers who had time still had them.

Space, not count. Long items save more page per cut.

What broke first

My first pass tagged difficulty using Bloom's taxonomy: remember is easy, analyse is hard. It looked tidy and it was wrong for these classrooms. Questions I had marked "middle" were ones these learners would find hard, so the mix came out skewed.

I rebuilt the scale around something the learner actually experiences: how far the answer sits from the textbook text.

The taxonomy was right. The calibration was wrong.

Artefact: the difficulty scale, before and after

LOT, MOT and HOT mean lower, middle and higher order thinking. Before, by thinking verb:

  • LOTRemember
  • MOTUnderstand, apply
  • HOTAnalyse, evaluate, create

After, by distance from the text

  • LOTThe answer is stated in the text and recalled
  • MOTThe answer is pieced together from the text
  • HOTThe answer is not spelled out anywhere in the text

Looking back

What worked

Writing the rules down before touching a question. The rules file and changelog are written so someone else can pick up the next chapter.

What I'd change

I calibrated the difficulty scale on my own and fixed it after. Next time I'd sit with two or three teachers from the schools before tagging a single question.

Case 3 · Curriculum design · Grades 1 to 5

Teaching six-year-olds what AI is, without calling it a brain

My role curriculum design, writing guide, authoring workflowAudience children aged 6 to 10 and their teachersStatus Grade 1 Units 1 to 3 drafted

The problem

The company wanted an AI Literacy series that starts in Grade 1, earlier than most curricula and ahead of national plans to introduce AI from Class 3. There was very little to copy from.

The trap I wanted to avoid

Children's material about AI tends to fail in one of two ways. Either AI is a magic brain, or it's made to look silly in contrived scenes. Both teach the wrong thing. I wanted children to see it as capable but limited.

One reference book called AI a computer brain. That was the first thing I ruled out.

Decisions I made

I planned all five grades before writing a single chapter, so that each year builds on the last. Every concept is discovered by doing before it gets a name. Formal vocabulary waits until Grade 3. Questions ask children to apply an idea, never to repeat what the page just said. Each step is pitched just beyond what they can already do, following Vygotsky's zone of proximal development.

When a reference book's format was useful but its claim broke our rules, I kept the format, rejected the claim, and wrote down which and why. That note became part of the writing guide.

Name it after they've met it, not before.

Where AI fits, and where it doesn't

Drafts are written by an AI workflow I set up, but it works to my writing guide, and every unit must pass a scored review (20 of 24) and a logic check before I read it. I decide what goes in; the tool saves the typing.

Looking back

What worked

Benchmarking the sequence against AI4K12, Code.org and Common Sense Education before writing. It settled arguments early.

What I'd change

I'd test Unit 1 with a real Grade 1 class before drafting Units 2 and 3. Until I've watched six-year-olds use it, I'm guessing.

Before It Goes Out: checking AI-written content

My role storyboard, scenario writing and buildAudience non-technical staff who use AI draftsLength about 6 minutes

Your manager wants an AI-written customer newsletter sent by 5 PM. You can send it straight away, or check it first. Send it unchecked and a customer's reply shows you what went wrong. Then you work through four planted problems: an invented health claim, a fake study, jargon for older readers and a customer's personal details. Each choice gets feedback that explains the consequence, and you need 3 out of 4 to "send" the newsletter.

Built with branching, triggers, feedback layers for each choice, a scored results slide and a retry path. All company and customer details are invented.
